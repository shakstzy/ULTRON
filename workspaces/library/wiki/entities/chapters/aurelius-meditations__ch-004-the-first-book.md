## TL;DR

Aurelius opens by listing every person who shaped him and exactly what trait he absorbed from each — grandfather, mother, tutors, brother, adopted father, the gods. It is a debt ledger written in public. The framing move is striking: before he says anything about how to live, he names where the parts came from.

## Key takeaways

- Run the credit audit. Naming who taught you what locks the lesson in and kills the illusion that you built yourself.
- Specificity beats sentiment. "From Rusticus I learned not to write tracts to show off virtue" hits harder than "Rusticus was a great teacher."
- The traits he picked are unfashionable on purpose: meekness, plain speech, refusing to be flattered, dropping rhetoric, not being theatrically busy. Quiet, hard things.
- He thanks the gods for what didn't happen as much as what did — youthful excesses he was spared, fights he never started. The dog that didn't bark counts.
- Notice how often the lesson is restraint: not collecting quail, not picking a chariot faction, not walking around the house in fancy robes. Most growth is subtraction.
- He closes with the morning script — today you will meet rude people, ignorant people, ungrateful people. None of them can actually harm you because you all share reason. Pre-arming the day against irritation.

## Quote

"To be neither a superstitious worshipper of the gods, nor an ambitious pleaser of men." — Aurelius, Book I, trans. Long

## Why it matters

A working blueprint for the people-in-my-life page nobody writes. Worth running once a year — list the trait, list the source, see what's missing.
