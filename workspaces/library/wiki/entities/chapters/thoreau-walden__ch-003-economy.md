## TL;DR

The longest chapter, and the load-bearing one. Thoreau runs the numbers on what it costs to be alive — literal cabin-build receipts, food bills, hours worked — and argues most "necessary" expenditure is socially-imposed superfluity. Every luxury is paid for in days of your one life.

## Key takeaways

- The four real necessaries: food, shelter, clothing, fuel. Everything else is a story you've been told about what a respectable person owns.
- He itemizes his cabin build at $28.12 and shows it covers shelter for years. The point isn't frugality theater — it's that the gap between needed and aspired-to is the whole prison.
- "The cost of a thing is the amount of life you trade for it." A coat that takes a week of labor costs a week of your life, no matter what the price tag reads.
- Inherited property is a trap, not a head start. The young men who inherit farms spend their lives serving the farm instead of being served by it.
- Voluntary poverty is the only vantage point for honest observation. Wealth distorts the view, and so does scarcity. The middle ground — owning little, owing nothing — is the philosopher's posture.
- Most "philanthropy" is paying to feel good about a system you helped build. Better to live so that no one needed your charity.

## Quote

"The cost of a thing is the amount of what I will call life which is required to be exchanged for it." — Thoreau, Walden ch. 1 (Economy)

## Why it matters

This is the original opportunity-cost framing applied to whole lifestyles, not just purchases. Adithya's own bias toward optionality, low fixed costs, and ownership-light living traces directly here. Re-read when an upgrade or a commitment starts looking like a default.
