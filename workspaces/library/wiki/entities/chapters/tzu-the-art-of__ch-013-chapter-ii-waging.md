## TL;DR

The chapter titled "Waging War" is mostly about money. Before you fight, count what fighting costs. Then commit to speed, because slow campaigns lose even when they win battles.

## Key takeaways

- Run the cost model first. Sun Tzu literally itemizes silver per day for 100,000 men — chariots, armor, supply lines, even glue. If you cannot price the operation, you cannot afford it.
- Drag = death. Long campaigns dull weapons, drain morale, exhaust the treasury, and invite third parties to attack you while you are tied up. "Stupid haste" wins more often than "clever delay."
- A skilled operator does not double back for reinforcements or fresh supplies. The first commit is the budget; cross the frontier and execute.
- Forage on the enemy. One unit of resource captured locally is worth twenty hauled from home, because logistics consumes itself in transit. Use what the field gives you.
- Distance is expensive twice: shipping supplies from home impoverishes the state, and the army's presence inflates local prices, gutting the people who live near it. Both ends of the supply chain bleed.
- Convert captured assets immediately. Take their chariots, fly your flags on them, treat captured soldiers well, and add them to your strength. The win pays for the next move.
- Soldiers fight on anger and rewards. Both have to be set up before the engagement, not improvised after.

## Quote

> "In war, then, let your great object be victory, not lengthy campaigns."
> — Sun Tzu, ch. II, trans. Giles

## Why it matters

The whole "move fast, take territory, monetize what you capture, never overextend supply lines" pattern Adithya already uses in product and music is right here. Speed is not aesthetic; it is the only way the math works.
